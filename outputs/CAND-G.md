   - A strong answer shows: Genuine ability to tolerate ambiguity by demonstrating: (1) a concrete example with real details, not a generic story; (2) acknowledgment of initial assumptions that proved incomplete or wrong; (3) the specific trigger or evidence that prompted reframing—not just hindsight; (4) tangible changes in how they then proceeded, showing the reframe wasn't cosmetic; (5) comfort with the discomfort of uncertainty mid-project rather than rushing to false clarity early on.

**2. [high_polish_low_artifact]** target: _Originality: Non-obvious or divergent approach to agentic system design_
   - Q: Walk me through a specific design decision you made in your agentic system that differed from how you'd seen it done in existing frameworks or papers. What trade-offs did you accept by going that route, and what made you believe it was worth the risk?
   - A strong answer shows: Deep familiarity with the actual constraints and failure modes of conventional agentic architectures, plus evidence of deliberate reasoning about why the unconventional choice solved a real problem—not just aesthetic novelty. A strong answer names concrete costs (e.g., harder to debug, slower onboarding, trickier to compose with standard tools) and articulates why those costs were acceptable for their use case.

**3. [unverified_scope]** target: _Reusability & Leverage: Abstraction and component library contributions to shared stack_
   - Q: Walk me through a specific component or abstraction you contributed to the shared library. What problem were you trying to solve for other teams, and how did you decide what to generalize versus what to keep specific to your use case?
   - A strong answer shows: Genuine understanding of the tension between reusability and specificity. A strong answer reveals concrete knowledge of: the actual constraints and edge cases they encountered, how they validated that other teams would benefit, trade-offs they made in API design, and evidence of actual reuse (adoption, feedback, or iteration). Weak answers lack specifics, conflate 'we made it a component' with 'it was reused,' or show they didn't think through generalization decisions.

**4. [claim_without_evidence]** target: _Ingenuity under constraint: Resourceful problem-solving or clever workarounds_
   - Q: Tell me about a time when you didn't have the tools, budget, or permissions you ideally would have needed to solve a problem. Walk me through what you actually did—what constraints did you identify first, and how did you decide between accepting the limitation versus finding a workaround?
   - A strong answer shows: Genuine resourcefulness requires the candidate to distinguish between creative problem-solving and mere corner-cutting. A strong answer reveals: (1) specific constraints they identified and why they mattered, (2) explicit reasoning about which constraints were immovable vs. negotiable, (3) acknowledgment of trade-offs or risks in their workaround, and (4) evidence they understood the underlying goal well enough to solve for it differently rather than just hacking a solution. Weak answers vaguely praise their own cleverness without naming the actual constraint or showing judgment about when workarounds are appropriate.

**5. [depth_probe]** target: _Enterprise Fluency: Priority negotiation and stakeholder complexity navigation_
   - Q: Walk me through a specific situation where you had to negotiate priorities with stakeholders who wanted conflicting things. What made you choose to prioritize one stakeholder's need over another's, and how did you handle the one whose request you deprioritized?
   - A strong answer shows: Genuine experience navigating stakeholder complexity: the candidate demonstrates concrete knowledge of trade-off reasoning, articulates the actual criteria used (business impact, timeline, dependencies, risk), shows awareness of relationship management beyond the immediate decision, and reveals learning or adjustment based on outcomes—not just a smooth resolution, but honest grappling with competing demands.
